Day 15 · The problem

A trial has two clocks. Both run out quietly.

A trial ends when the days run out or when the shipments run out, whichever comes first. If nobody is watching, a good lead goes cold and the customer just finds a locked account.

01

Sales can't see the whole picture

Who is on a trial, how many days and shipments are left, and who is about to expire. Without one place for this, sales has to ask around or keep their own notes.

02

Customers go quiet

Some customers get access and never sign in. By the time anyone notices, the trial is over and the chance is gone.

03

The end should not feel like a wall

When the trial ends, the customer should know why and who to contact. Not just hit an error.

Day 12 · Who it's for

Three people. Each needs different news at a different time.

Before drawing screens, I mapped who sees what. The same event, like "trial ending soon", means "follow up now" for sales and "talk to us to keep going" for the customer.

Where I learned this: I talked to the sales team, who run the trials, and worked from the PM's requirements. I didn't talk to operations or to trial customers before launch.

S
SalespersonInternal admin

Creates the trial, watches it, extends it or converts it to a paid account. Needs to know when to call.

O
Operations teamInternal

Sets up the account behind the scenes. Needs to know a new trial exists, and nothing more.

C
CustomerSuper admin of the trial account

Tries the platform. Needs to know how to sign in, what's included, and when it ends.

Day 9 · The journey

10 emails, but each one has one job.

Every email answers one question and ends with one action: sign in, view the customer, or contact sales. Account details sit in the same card in every email so people know where to look.

  1. Day 1

    Trial created

    SalesOpsCustomer

    Customer gets the sign-in link, features included and the end date. Password comes separately from sales.

  2. Day 5, no sign-in

    We haven't seen you

    SalesCustomer

    Sales gets a subject line that starts with "ACTION". The customer gets a soft nudge, not a warning.

  3. Near the end

    Ending soon

    SalesCustomer

    Sales gets a "View customer detail" button. The customer is told how to keep going.

  4. Any time

    Extended

    Customer

    Good news leads: the new end date is in the first line, then the usual account card.

  5. Day 15 or 0 shipments

    Trial ended

    SalesCustomer

    Customer gets one button: Contact sales. Sales is asked to reach out now.

Small choices in the welcome email

  • End date up front. It's highlighted in yellow inside the account card, not hidden at the bottom.
  • No password in the email. It's sent separately by the salesperson, and the email says so, so nobody thinks it's missing.
  • Features as tiles. Four short tiles show what's included, so the customer knows what to try first.
  • A real person to contact. The email ends with the salesperson's own email, not a generic inbox.

Day 6 · Key decisions

What sales sees, and what the customer sees.

01

One list for every trial. Both clocks side by side, in colour.

Freemium customers get their own page, separate from paying customers. Each row shows days remaining and shipments left, with a small bar that turns from green to orange to red.

Plenty leftHalf goneAlmost out
WhyA salesperson scanning 20 rows should spot who to call without reading every number. On the last day the text itself says "Expires Today" in red, so it doesn't depend on colour alone.
02

Extend without leaving the page. Show what's left before asking what to add.

"Extend" opens a small popup from the row. The top of the popup repeats the current state, so the salesperson decides with the numbers in front of them. They can add days, shipments, or both.

WhyThe success message says exactly what changed: "Freemium trial has been extended by 5 days and 5 shipments for Global Imports." The row updates in place, and the customer gets the "Extended" email with the new date.
03

See exactly what the customer sees, with a clear "you are inside their account" bar.

When a customer says "I can't find it", sales can open the platform as that customer. They pick which user to view, a short loading screen confirms it, and a dark bar stays at the top the whole time: "Viewing: Swift Cargo Inc. (James Deo)" with a Close button.

WhyActing inside someone else's account is risky. The bar never scrolls away, so a salesperson can't forget whose account they are in.
04

One page per trial, with everything sales needs before a call.

Clicking a customer opens their trial page: dates, both clocks, services and modules included, sign-in email, and which salesperson owns the account. The three actions sit together at the top: Extend, Convert, and Open the customer's platform.

WhyBefore a sales call you want one screen, not three tabs. The owner is shown so a teammate knows who to hand the lead back to.
05

When the trial ends: a clear message, not a locked door.

The customer can still sign in and see their account. A red banner across the top says the trial has ended and who to contact. Each trial shipment uses credits, so when the 10 shipments are used up, the credits page says it plainly: "Not enough credits left to process shipments."

WhyLocking someone out loses the lead. Showing them their account, with one way forward, keeps the conversation open for sales.
06

Even the empty screens say something.

On day one there are no trials yet, so the page says so in plain words. When a search finds nothing, the filters stay on screen so it's easy to change them.

Day 3 · Rules I followed

Four ideas that shaped every screen.

01

Map who sees what first

Three audiences, five events. The table came before any email or screen.

02

Two limits, one look

Days and shipments always appear together, with the same bar style.

03

Every message has one action

Sign in, view customer, or contact sales. Never a list of links.

04

Never leave people stuck

Ended trials still open, with a banner and a person to contact.

Day 0 · Outcome

One place to run every trial, from first email to last day.

The work covered 3 audiences, 10 lifecycle emails, the sales admin pages and the customer-side states for a trial that is active, extended or ended.

What I learned

A trial is a conversation between sales and the customer. The product just keeps it going.

  • The same event needs different words. "Ending soon" is a to-do for sales and an invitation for the customer.
  • Limits need a shape. Showing 5/20 instead of only "5 left" made extensions visible at a glance.
  • Next time, I'd agree on the success numbers with the PM before launch, so the outcome above could have real figures.
