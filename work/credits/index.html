The backstory

Credits were added by hand, and paid for later.

Credits work like a prepaid travel card: importers spend them on customs services. The problem was how they got topped up.

Where I learned this: the finance team was chasing late credit invoices, sales and account managers had no way to offer discounts, and the product manager brought the business goal of self-serve, pay-up-front credits. I didn’t talk to customers directly while designing it.

The goal

Let customers serve themselves, and get paid up front.

The admin creates offers and the customer's finance manager buys credits. The fix had to work for both, and for cash flow.

Constraints

  • Stripe holds all payment data (PCI DSS Level 1). The UI had to say so simply.
  • Three ways to pay: card, ACH bank transfer and offline, and each fails differently.
  • Existing design system, including its built-in input error state.
  • 2–4 weeks of design as the only designer, inside the Jun–Jul 2024 project.
  • Business rules were already set. Who can create promo codes and the discount limits were decided by the business. My job was to make those rules clear in the UI and hard to misuse.

Admin · Decision A

Targeting and limits are built into the form.

Admins choose Country specific or Customer specific in the form itself, then set a percent or credit discount, start and end dates, and a usage limit like "Only once". Customers can apply only one promo code per order, so discounts never stack.

?A discount is real money. Limits stop overuse, and targeting covers both regional campaigns and key-account deals.
Admin · Decision B

Every code has a home: status, actions and history.

Each code shows its availability, discount, dates and an Active switch, with quick actions to edit, clone or send, and a Redeem history with Excel export.

?Clone saves retyping similar offers, and the history answers "who used this, and what did it cost?"
Admin · Decision C

Discounts can grow with the order.

Codes can use one rate, like BIGFRIDAYSALE (2% at every order size), or be tiered. With Configure Tiers, admins set ranges, like TRADEQUICK: 2% up to 1,000 credits, 3% up to 2,000 and 4% up to 3,000.

?Without tiers, admins would need a separate code for each order size.
⇄Trade-off: admins do more setup when creating a code, and tiers are harder to read than one number.
The checkout

One page, three steps, and the money is always visible.

Follow a finance manager buying 5,000 credits, one decision at a time.

Checkout · Decision 1

Steps on one page, not separate pages.

Credits, Address and Payment sit on one page. A finished step folds into one line with a Change link, and the order summary stays in view.

?Business buyers want to see everything before paying. Nothing hides on a previous page, and fixing a mistake is one click.
Checkout · Decision 2

Buy exactly what you need, in one tap.

This replaces "please add credits". Customers type any amount or tap a preset. Bigger bundles cost less per credit: 1 USD up to 5,000 credits, then 0.95 at 10,000, 0.90 at 25,000, down to 0.60 at 200,000. An info icon opens the full price table. A promo code is applied after volume pricing: in the example, 5,000 credits at 1 USD is 5,000 USD, and BIGFRIDAYSALE (a 2% code) takes off 100 USD.

?Customers used to ask for a number; now they choose it. Presets cover common amounts.
Checkout · Decision 3

Pick a promo from a list instead of guessing a code.

Apply Promo Code opens the offers this customer can use. They can still type a code. Picking one shows a "$100 saved" moment.

?People can only use offers they can see. The savings moment confirms the discount before any money moves.
⇄Trade-off: showing offers can train customers to wait for a discount before buying. We limited the list to offers each customer is eligible for, instead of advertising every campaign.
What I changed

I started with a code box, then switched to a list.

My first version put a small box on the page where customers typed a promo code. Sales told us customers kept asking which codes they could use, so a box that needs you to already know the code didn't work. I replaced it with a popup that lists every offer the customer can use, so they pick one instead of guessing.

Checkout · Decision 4

Pay now, the way finance teams pay.

This replaces "pay the invoice later": Card, Bank transfer (ACH) or Pay offline. Saved cards show only the last four digits, and a "Powered by Stripe" note says where card details go. Card payments add credits right away. ACH and offline payments still happen before credits are used, but credits wait until the payment is confirmed, and the customer sees a "credits pending" state in the meantime.

?Paying up front only works if companies can pay the way they already do, and large B2B amounts often can't go on a card.
⇄Trade-off: ACH and offline customers wait longer for credits than card customers. We accepted slower credits rather than adding credits before the money has cleared.
Checkout · Decision 5

The order summary is a live receipt.

The summary updates at every step: credits, promo discount, subtotal, the Stripe fee added on top, and the final total. Preview invoice shows the document before paying.

?The old invoice arrived after the fact. Now every line is visible before paying, including who charges the fee.

The payoff

Paid, and the credits are there (or clearly on their way).

A short processing screen leads to a clear receipt with Download invoice and a way back to purchase history. When an ACH or offline payment hasn't cleared yet, the customer sees "Payment received, credits are pending" instead, with a plain "Do not pay again."

When things go wrong

26 ways a payment can fail. None of them should feel scary.

A confusing payment error makes people pay twice, call support, or go back to emailing the team. For each failure I asked: how much of the page does this affect?

Anatomy of every error
1

What happened, in plain words: "Pricing could not be refreshed."

2

What's safe, because money makes people nervous: "Your quantity is saved… no payment has been submitted."

3

One next step, written as an action: "Retry pricing". When the payment result is unknown, that step is "Check payment status", never "Pay again", so nobody is charged twice.

Accessible by default

  • The error red passes WCAG AA (#D92D20, about 4.8:1 on white), even for 12px helper text.
  • Never colour alone. Every error has a written message, not only a red border.
  • Errors sit next to the problem, not somewhere else on the page.
⇄Trade-off: 26 designed states cost more build time than a generic "Something went wrong". We accepted that because with money, a vague error makes people pay twice or call support.

Outcome

Shipped: credits on demand, paid up front.

Customers now top up on their own, credits are paid for at purchase, and admins run their own offers. In the first 4–5 months after launch, about 6–7% of customers started buying credits through the new Stripe checkout. Every customer could use it, and this was what the PM expected. I checked the number with the PM; it is an estimate, not tracked analytics. Here is what I'd measure to understand it better.

What I'd validate if I revisited it

I didn't test this flow with customers before launch. If I revisited it, this is the test I'd run first: a 30-minute remote session with 5 finance managers who buy credits today.

  • Task 1: Buy 5,000 credits with the best promo you can find. Success: they find the list and can explain the total before paying.
  • Task 2: Your card is declined. What do you do? Success: they choose another method without leaving checkout.
  • Task 3: You paid by ACH yesterday and credits aren't there. Success: they understand "pending" and don't pay again.

What I learned

Changing how people pay is a trust problem, not only a screen problem.

  • Paying up front needs total clarity. With "pay later", customers could check the invoice first. Now the receipt must be clear before they pay.
  • Severity decides placement. My first pass put a red banner on every error, and the flow looked broken. Field, section and page levels made it calm.
  • Next time, I'd test with real finance managers before launch and agree on success metrics with the PM on day one.
